## A.1 Mechanised work and the Luddites (1811–1817)

**Source claim:** E.P. Thompson, *The Making of the English Working Class* (1963), argued that the Luddites were not mindless machine-breakers but participants in a “moral economy” — a pre-capitalist framework of reciprocal obligation in which the price of bread and the wage of labour were understood as moral questions, not merely market outcomes. Thompson explicitly fought against what he called “the enormous condescension of posterity,” the tendency to dismiss Luddite resistance as irrational.

**My reading against the author’s view:** This supports point 1 (origin from judgment and reflection) and point 4 (inefficiency as human). The Luddites acted from their own judgment about what was just, not from expert recommendation. Their resistance was inefficient by the standards of industrial rationality — which is precisely the author’s point. It complicates point 6 (drift through comfort) because the Luddites did not drift; they actively resisted. The author’s framework may underestimate the possibility of conscious refusal.

**Contested evidence:** The Luddites’ relationship to Romanticism is debated. Some scholars argue that the Luddites were not anti-technology per se but were defending customary rights and wages. The “enormous condescension of posterity” framing is Thompson’s, and later historians have questioned whether the moral economy was as coherent as he suggested.

---

## A.2 Industrial time discipline (c. 1750–1850)

**Source claim:** Thompson’s 1967 essay “Time, Work-Discipline, and Industrial Capitalism” argued that the introduction of mechanical clocks into factories produced a “restructuring of working habits” and a change in the “inward notation of time.” Workers moved from task-orientation (working until the task was done) to time-orientation (working for a wage measured by the clock). Thompson explicitly argued that this was not caused by new manufacturing techniques alone but was an effect of wider changes in the organisation of production.

**My reading against the author’s view:** This directly supports point 4. The pre-industrial task-orientation allowed for the “forty-minute walk home” — time that was not measured, not optimised, and in which reflection could occur. Clock-time made such inefficiency irrational. The author’s point that “efficiency and rationality are not romance” is historically grounded in this transformation. The wave did not “take” romance directly; it took the temporal conditions in which reflection flourished.

**Contested evidence:** Later scholars have argued that Thompson’s picture is incomplete. Capital flows and itinerant labour complicate the linear narrative of a single transition from task-orientation to clock-time. The transition was uneven and contested.

---

## A.3 Railways and the telegraph (1830s–1884)

**Source claim:** Zerubavel’s “The Standardization of Time: A Sociohistorical Perspective” (1982) argued that the rise of standard time was driven by the establishment of national and international communication networks following railway transportation and telegraphic communication. The laying of the Atlantic cable in 1866 created pressure for an international time system, and the 1884 International Meridian Conference agreed to a system keyed to Greenwich Mean Time. Standard time dissociated time from nature and placed it under the authority of the network.

Ishibashi’s (2014) study of Greenwich time signals shows that the Royal Observatory’s time service was used for railway operation, time balls and time guns. The nationalisation of telegraph lines in 1870 changed how the service was maintained, but the signals remained the basis for accurate public timekeeping and the widespread use of standard time.

**My reading against the author’s view:** This supports point 7 (gathering and deliberation) and point 4 (inefficiency). Before railways, each locality had its own time, tied to the sun. The railway and telegraph replaced this local, embodied time with a single abstract time. The “forty-minute walk” becomes possible only when time is not yet fully standardised. The telegraph also changed exchange: it made communication instantaneous but disembodied, prefiguring the author’s concern with AI-mediated conversation.

**Contested evidence:** On Barak’s work on colonial Egypt argues that technological modernity did not drive a single standardised time-consciousness but triggered “countertempos” — alternative timekeeping practices that subverted the modernist infatuation with promptness. This complicates any simple narrative of loss.

## A.4 Photography and recorded sound (1830s–1900)

**Source claim:** Walter Benjamin, in “The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction” (1935), argued that the reproduction of a work of art through photography or a gramophone record depreciates its authenticity — its “hic et nunc” — and destroys its “aura.” The aura is defined as “a singular web of time and space: the unique appearance of a distance, however close it may be.” Mechanical reproduction makes the original ubiquitous but no longer unique. Benjamin saw this as both loss and possibility: the erosion of aura is bound up with the democratisation of production and reception.

**My reading against the author’s view:** This supports point 1 (origin from a person’s own judgment and reflection) in a modified form. Benjamin’s aura is not the same as romance, but both involve a relation to origin: the authentic, the irreplaceable, the presence that cannot be reproduced. The author says the medium does not matter (letters, texts and favours are the same). Benjamin suggests the medium does matter: mechanical reproduction changes the relation between the work and its origin, and therefore changes what is possible in the encounter. This complicates the author’s indifference to medium.

**Contested evidence:** Susan Buck-Morss argues that Benjamin did not regard the erosion of aura as simply negative; he saw the democratisation of production and reception as progressive. The question of whether the loss of aura is a loss at all remains contested.

## A.5 The telephone (1876–1970s)

**Source claim:** Robert Putnam, in “Bowling Alone” (1995) and *Bowling Alone* (2000), argued that social capital in America declined sharply from the 1960s onward. He identified television as a major cause, but also noted that the telephone — particularly the shift from long-distance to local and cellular calling — may have displaced face-to-face contact. Putnam’s evidence is correlational and has been widely debated.

**My reading against the author’s view:** This partially supports point 7 (gathering) and point 2 (exchange as mutual bonds). The telephone allows exchange without gathering. For the author, exchange requires mutual bonds that arise through owing each other. The telephone may allow the maintenance of bonds across distance, but it does not create the conditions of gathering in which deliberation and back-and-forth occur. The telephone is a medium of exchange, not of gathering.

**Contested evidence:** Other scholars argue that the telephone strengthened social capital by allowing people to maintain weak ties and family connections across distance. The evidence is mixed. Putnam himself acknowledged that the telephone’s effects are difficult to disentangle from other changes.

---

## A.6 The automobile and suburbs (1920s–1960s)

**Source claim:** Andrea Vesentini, *Indoor America: The Interior Landscape of Postwar Suburbia* (2018), argues that the encapsulation of the automobile fostered the nuclear segregation of the family from the social fabric. The enclosed automobile cabin segregated the driver from the heterogeneous and racially diverse world of the city; cars became “living rooms on wheels.” Introverted design turned the focus of the house inward, and the relocation of everyday practices into indoor spaces created a landscape of “interconnected and self-sufficient escape capsules.”

Kunstler (1993, `probable`) makes a related argument: post-war suburbanisation, urban renewal, residential mobility, auto dependency, freeway expansion and single-use zoning contributed to the disappearance of informal gathering spaces. Newer suburban subdivisions failed to provide spaces for community life, largely due to the increasing isolation of family life and the extreme individualisation championed by American society.

**My reading against the author’s view:** This directly supports point 7. The suburb is the spatial form of gathering’s decline. The “forty-minute walk” becomes impossible when walking is replaced by driving and the destination is a private house rather than a public square. The automobile supports point 4 (inefficiency as human) in reverse: it made inefficiency unacceptable by making efficiency the default.

**Contested evidence:** Some scholars argue that suburbanisation had complex effects: it gave people privacy, home ownership and autonomy, which were genuine goods. The author’s framework does not name these goods, which is a gap.

---

## A.7 Radio and television (1920s–1980s)

**Source claim:** Putnam (1995) argued that the rise of television may be responsible for social capital’s decline. Television keeps people at home, displacing the social activities — bowling leagues, civic associations, church groups — that once built social capital. Olken (2006) tested this hypothesis in Indonesian villages using variation in signal strength and found that better reception was associated with substantially lower participation in social activities and lower self-reported trust. The effect was particularly strong for participation in local government activities and informal savings groups.

**My reading against the author’s view:** This supports point 7 and point 3 (the four balances declining). Television, like the algorithmic feed, is a medium of passive reception rather than active exchange. The author’s point that “a conversation among gathered people whose source is ‘the AI said…’ is not romantic” has a predecessor here: a family gathered around a television is not gathered in the author’s sense, because the source of the conversation is not their own judgment and reflection but the broadcast.

**Contested evidence:** The evidence is contested. Brookes (2004, `probable`) argued that Putnam’s analysis is one-sided and that the relationship between television and social capital is more complex. Uslaner and others have questioned the causal direction. The Indonesian evidence is strong but may not generalise.

---

## A.8 Mass consumer culture (1920s–1970s)

**Source claim:** Horkheimer and Adorno, in *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (1944/2002), argued that the “culture industry” produces standardised, packaged cultural commodities that pacify consumers and reproduce the existing order. Culture becomes a commodity governed by exchange value rather than use value. The culture industry promises escape from everyday life but never delivers it; it “unceasingly promises” something that is “actually a treacherous construction.”

Marcuse (1964, `probable`) argued that consumer culture produces “false needs” that bind individuals to the existing system and prevent critical reflection.

**My reading against the author’s view:** This supports point 3 (balances declining) and point 6 (drift through comfort). The culture industry is a precursor to the algorithmic feed: it offers comfort, distraction and the illusion of choice, while systematically undermining the conditions for critical reflection. The author’s Circe metaphor is essentially the culture industry thesis updated for personalised media.

**Contested evidence:** Later scholars have questioned the passivity that Horkheimer and Adorno attribute to consumers. Edgar and Sedgwick note that the Frankfurt School themselves “do not, for example, obviously assume that human subjects are passive victims of the culture industry.” Consumption of cultural products is diverse and can be active.

## A.9 Personal computer and internet (1980s–2000s)

**Source claim:** Kern (2025) argues that the proliferation of smartphones and social media presents serious challenges for social capital in America, contradicting the optimistic predictions of the late 1990s that the internet would boost social capital. The formation of sustainable civic groups has decreased with the increase of ephemeral activist networks. Early studies suggested a negative impact of information technology on social life; later studies found that internet use was not consistently correlated with lower levels of socialising.

**My reading against the author’s view:** This supports point 7 and point 3. The internet gave people access to information and new weak-tie networks, but the early optimism that it would restore social capital did not materialise. The author’s point that exchange requires mutual bonds is relevant: online networks can maintain weak ties, but they do not necessarily create the obligations and debts that the author sees as constitutive of love, understanding and trust.

**Contested evidence:** Studies from other latitudes show that internet use can increase social capital by broadening and diversifying discussion networks. The evidence is mixed and context-dependent.

---

## A.10 Mobile phones (1990s–2010s)

**Source claim:** Sherry Turkle, *Reclaiming Conversation* (2015), argues that smartphones and devices are changing not only what we do but who we are — and not for the better. Based on fieldwork with hundreds of children and adults, Turkle concluded that over-reliance on digital communication can result in feelings of real-world isolation and loneliness, emotional disconnection, anxiety and mental exhaustion. Her summation is “We are forever elsewhere.”

**My reading against the author’s view:** This directly supports point 2 (exchange as mutual bonds) and point 7 (gathering). The phone allows exchange without presence. Turkle’s “forever elsewhere” is the phenomenological correlate of the author’s claim that AI scatters people into individualism. The phone is a device that allows connection but not gathering.

**Contested evidence:** Turkle’s work is qualitative and has been criticised for relying on anecdote rather than representative data. The evidence for a causal link between phones and loneliness is weaker than her argument suggests.

---

## A.11 Social media and smartphones (2007–2020)

**Source claim:** Jonathan Haidt, *The Anxious Generation* (2024), argues that the “Great Rewiring” took place between 2010 and 2015, when youth smartphone and internet use escalated from rare to ubiquitous. Haidt attributes rising rates of adolescent anxiety and depression to the pervasive influence of social media and smartphones, which have eroded in-person socialising, play and sleep. He proposes four new norms to “restore childhood,” including limiting access to smartphones and social media for under-fourteens.

**My reading against the author’s view:** This supports point 5 (the test for AI: gather or scatter) and point 3 (balances declining). Haidt’s “phone-based childhood” is a scattered childhood: children are connected to networks but disconnected from the gathered community of play. The author’s point that mistakes and inefficiency are human is relevant: play is inefficient, but it is how people learn to be with each other.

**Contested evidence:** This is one of the most contested areas in the literature. Candice Odgers argues that “science to date does not support the widespread panic around social media and mental health.” The effects are heterogeneous, depending on individual, content and context. Researchers agree that adolescents are struggling, but there is fierce debate about how much phones and social media are to blame.

---

## A.12 Algorithmic feeds (2009–2020)

**Source claim:** Eli Pariser, *The Filter Bubble* (2011), coined the term to describe the “unique, personal universe of information” created for each user by personalising filters. Pariser argued that personalisation isolates users from new discoveries and insights that fall outside their usual tastes, and that the filter bubble is invisible — users do not know what they are missing.

**My reading against the author’s view:** This supports point 6 (drift through comfort). The algorithmic feed is the author’s Circe: it offers comfort, relevance and the illusion of agency while narrowing the range of what is encountered. The author’s point that the market’s floor is mutual belief and credit is relevant here: algorithmic feeds trade on attention and engagement, not on the reciprocal obligations that constitute credit.

**Contested evidence:** Bruns (2019) provides a critical review of the filter bubble idea, arguing that Pariser fails to provide a clear definition and that the persistence of the concept has redirected scholarly attention from more critical areas of enquiry. The empirical evidence for filter bubbles is mixed. Some studies find evidence of echo chambers; others do not.

## A.13 How credit itself changed

**Source claim:** Olegario, *A Culture of Credit* (2006), traces the transformation of business credit from a system based largely on information circulating through personal networks to one dependent on formalised methods and institutions. The credit-reporting agency first appeared in the 1830s. Later, groups of creditors formed interchanges and bureaus to share payment records. By 1920, credit men had established a national credit information clearinghouse. These developments forced businesses to make their financial situations more transparent to creditors and reporting firms.

**My reading against the author’s view:** This supports point 8 (the market’s floor is mutual belief and credit). Credit is etymologically and morally linked to belief — both from the Latin *credere*. The transformation Olegario describes is the transformation of credit from a relation between persons to a relation between a person and an institution. The author’s point that love, understanding and trust arise only by owing each other is a claim about personal credit; the institutional credit system is a different order of relation.

**Contested evidence:** Scholars of credit markets argue that personal and impersonal trust coexisted rather than simply replacing one another. The shift from personal to impersonal trust was uneven and complementary.


# B. The Author’s View Against Published Thought

## Point 1: Origin — something is romantic when its origin is a person’s own judgment and reflection

**Supports:**
- Benjamin, *Work of Art* (1935): authenticity depends on origin and presence; mechanical reproduction dissolves the relation to origin. `verified`
- Thompson, *Making of the English Working Class* (1963): the moral economy was grounded in the judgments of the people themselves, not in expert recommendation. `verified`
- Turkle, *Reclaiming Conversation* (2015): digital communication can provide “the illusion of companionship without the demands of friendship.” The demands of friendship are demands of judgment and reflection. `verified`

**Complicates or contradicts:**
- Horkheimer and Adorno, *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (1944): the culture industry produces the illusion of individual judgment. The “person’s own judgment” may itself be manufactured. `verified`
- Pariser, *The Filter Bubble* (2011): the algorithmic feed shapes what a person encounters before they exercise judgment. `verified`
- Schopenhauer, “Metaphysics of Sexual Love”: love is a fleeting illusion produced by the will, not by judgment. This is a philosophical objection to the author’s rationalism about romance. `probable`

**Strongest objection:** The author’s point assumes a sovereign judging subject whose judgments are genuinely their own. The Frankfurt School and the filter-bubble literature both suggest that modern subjects are constituted by forces that precede and shape their judgment. If the origin of judgment is not the person but the system, the author’s definition of romance is either empty or impossible.

**What the author’s view lacks:** The concept of *plurality* — Hannah Arendt’s claim that judgment is not solitary but requires the presence of others, the “enlarged mentality” that comes from representing the perspectives of others. The author says solitary reflection is romantic; Arendt would say that judgment becomes political and fully human only when it takes the others into account.

---

## Point 2: Exchange as mutual bonds — what people give and receive is issuing bonds to each other

**Supports:**
- Mauss, *The Gift* (1925): the obligation to give, to receive and to return sustains long-term social relationships. Reciprocal exchanges bind individuals and groups. `verified`
- Blau (1964): social interaction is a process of social exchange in which one person gives rewards and the other is obligated to reciprocate. `probable`
- Olegario, *A Culture of Credit* (2006): credit is linked to trust and belief; trust relates to meeting reciprocal expectations. `verified`

**Complicates or contradicts:**
- Horkheimer and Adorno: in the culture industry, exchange becomes commodity exchange. The bond is replaced by the transaction. `verified`
- Putnam, *Bowling Alone* (2000): social capital is a stock of reciprocal obligations, but it is declining. The bonds that Mauss describes are being replaced by impersonal transactions. `verified`
- Simmel, *Philosophy of Money* (1900, `probable`): money replaces personal bonds with impersonal calculation. The author’s point is essentially Simmel’s, but Simmel was less optimistic that the bonds could be preserved.

**Strongest objection:** The author’s model of exchange as mutual bonds assumes that all exchange creates bonds. But some exchange — market exchange, algorithmic exchange, AI-mediated exchange — dissolves bonds rather than creating them. The author does not distinguish between types of exchange. Mauss’s gift is not the same as a market transaction; the author’s framework risks flattening the distinction.

**What the author’s view lacks:** The distinction between *gift* and *commodity*, and the concept of *focal practice* (Borgmann). A focal practice is a practice that engages body, attention and community — cooking, walking, playing music. The author’s exchange is abstract; the literature on focal practices gives it concrete form. Mauss’s gift is never free: a giver imparts a part of oneself in the object. The author’s bonds are similarly constitutive, but the author does not specify what the objects of exchange are.

---

## Point 3: Balances — love, understanding, trust and belief are decreasing

**Supports:**
- Putnam (1995, 2000): social trust in America has fallen by more than a third since the early 1960s. `verified`
- Reuters Institute (2026): global trust in news has fallen to 37%; trust in news via social media and AI chatbots is 22% and 20%. `probable`
- Olken (2006): better television reception is associated with lower self-reported trust in Indonesian villages. `verified`

**Complicates or contradicts:**
- The evidence for a generalised decline in love, understanding and trust is weaker than the author suggests. Trust in some institutions has declined, but trust in others may have risen. The decline narrative is contested.
- Some scholars argue that the apparent decline is partly a measurement artefact: people are more aware of mental-health issues and more willing to report them.

**Strongest objection:** The author claims that the main problem is that the balances are decreasing, not that hatred and misunderstanding are increasing. But the empirical literature on polarisation suggests that hatred and misunderstanding *are* increasing, at least in some domains. The author’s framework may be too optimistic about the trajectory of the negative emotions.

**What the author’s view lacks:** The concept of *resonance* (Rosa). Rosa argues that late modern societies are characterised by the erosion of resonant relations with the world — relations in which subjects are moved and respond. The author’s “balances” are quantitative; resonance is qualitative. Rosa’s framework would say that the problem is not the quantity of trust but the quality of relation.

## Point 4: What is human — mistakes and inefficiency are human and romantic

**Supports:**
- Thompson, “Time, Work-Discipline” (1967): the pre-industrial task-orientation allowed for unmeasured, inefficient time. `verified`
- Thompson, *Making of the English Working Class* (1963): the Luddites resisted the rationalisation of their labour. `verified`
- Turkle, *Reclaiming Conversation* (2015): conversation requires inefficiency — pauses, tangents, silences. `verified`

**Complicates or contradicts:**
- Horkheimer and Adorno: the culture industry makes inefficiency impossible by colonising leisure time. `verified`
- The attention economy literature: platforms are engineered to capture attention, making inefficiency a resource to be extracted rather than a human good. `probable`

**Strongest objection:** The author treats inefficiency as an intrinsic good. But inefficiency can also be a form of waste, and the romanticisation of inefficiency can be a privilege. The person who takes the forty-minute walk has the time to do so. The author’s framework does not address the material conditions that make inefficiency possible.

**What the author’s view lacks:** The concept of *friction* (Tsing). Tsing argues that friction — the awkward, unequal, unstable and creative qualities of interconnection across difference — is what produces movement and effect. Friction is not simply inefficiency; it is the productive resistance that generates new possibilities. The author’s inefficiency is close to Tsing’s friction, but the author does not distinguish between wasted friction and generative friction.

---

## Point 5: A test for AI — does it gather people or scatter them?

**Supports:**
- Turkle: digital technology “can provide the illusion of companionship without the demands of friendship.” It scatters. `verified`
- Haidt: smartphones and social media have displaced play-based childhood. They scatter. `verified`
- Arendt: plurality — the presence of others — is the law of the earth. AI that replaces the presence of others is a threat to plurality. `verified`

**Complicates or contradicts:**
- Some studies suggest that AI-mediated communication can lower epistemic trust, but others suggest it can facilitate connection for isolated people. The effect depends on use. `probable`
- The question “does it gather or scatter?” is a binary. The literature suggests a spectrum.

**Strongest objection:** The author’s test assumes that gathering is always good and scattering always bad. But gathering can also be oppressive (the surveillance of the small town, the conformity of the crowd). Scattering can be liberating (escape from tradition, anonymity of the city). The author’s framework does not name these possibilities.

**What the author’s view lacks:** The concept of *algorithmic collectivism* — the idea that AI does not simply scatter individuals but creates new forms of algorithmic belonging that influence meaning and social coordination. The author’s binary misses this.

---

## Point 6: Drift through comfort — like Odysseus’s men on Circe’s island

**Supports:**
- Horkheimer and Adorno: the culture industry offers comfort and escape while reproducing the existing order. `verified`
- Pariser: the filter bubble creates a personalised comfort zone that narrows the range of encountered ideas. `verified`
- Algorithmic drift literature: recommender systems slowly reshape user preferences and identity. `probable`

**Complicates or contradicts:**
- The empirical evidence for filter bubbles is contested. Some studies find echo chambers; others find that online exposure is more diverse than offline. The drift metaphor may be too strong.
- The audience is not passive. Horkheimer and Adorno themselves did not assume passivity.

**Strongest objection:** The Circe metaphor implies that people are enchanted and do not know what they are losing. But people do know. There is widespread awareness of smartphone addiction, filter bubbles and algorithmic manipulation. The question is not whether they know but whether they can do anything about it. The author’s metaphor may underestimate agency.

**What the author’s view lacks:** The concept of *attention economy* as a political-economic structure. The problem is not just that people drift through comfort but that comfort is engineered by platforms whose business model depends on capturing attention. The author’s metaphor is psychological; the attention economy literature is structural.

---

## Point 7: Gathering — gathering, deliberating together and going back and forth is what made people human

**Supports:**
- Arendt: plurality, the presence of others, is the condition of political judgment. `verified`
- Evolutionary psychology: deliberation evolved as an adaptive response to the problem of intra-group political disagreements among hunter-gatherers. `probable`
- Putnam: the decline of civic associations, bowling leagues and church groups is the decline of gathering. `verified`
- Vesentini: suburbanisation removed informal gathering spaces. `verified`

**Complicates or contradicts:**
- Not all gathering is deliberative. Some gathering is hierarchical, ritualistic or coercive. The author’s gathering is idealised.
- Online gathering is still gathering. Some scholars argue that digital communities can be sites of deliberation, albeit in different forms. The author dismisses this too quickly.

**Strongest objection:** The author’s claim that gathering “is what made people human” is an empirical claim about human evolution that is difficult to substantiate. Deliberation may have evolved for intra-group conflict resolution, but that is not the same as the claim that gathering is constitutive of humanity.

**What the author’s view lacks:** The concept of *focal things and practices* (Borgmann). Gathering is not just a matter of people being together; it is a matter of engaging together with a focal thing — a meal, a musical performance, a shared task. The author’s gathering is abstract; Borgmann gives it a material centre.

---

## Point 8: The market’s floor — the financial market’s origin is mutual belief and credit

**Supports:**
- Olegario: credit is linked to trust; trust relates to reciprocal expectations. `verified`
- Card Carrying Christians (2017): credit and belief share the same Latin root, *credere*. `probable`
- Mauss: reciprocal exchanges bind individuals and groups. The market is a system of exchange, and exchange is a system of bonds. `verified`

**Complicates or contradicts:**
- Simmel, *Philosophy of Money*: money replaces personal bonds with impersonal calculation. The market’s floor may be belief, but the market also erodes the conditions of belief. `probable`
- The institutional credit literature: the transformation from personal credit networks to institutional credit reporting replaced trust with data. The market’s floor is now quantification, not mutual belief. `verified`

**Strongest objection:** The author claims that the financial market stands on mutual belief and credit. But the history of credit shows that the market has systematically replaced mutual belief with impersonal mechanisms — credit reports, ratings, algorithms. The floor may once have been belief; it is now something else.

**What the author’s view lacks:** The concept of *impersonal trust*. The author’s market is a market of persons who owe each other. The modern market is a market of institutions and algorithms. The author does not name the difference.


# C. Recurring Concepts — Proposals for an Ontology

The following concepts recur across the sources. They are my proposals for concepts that could feed an ontology. Each is accompanied by the sources from which it is drawn.

**1. Reciprocity** — The obligation to give, receive and return. Not simply exchange but the binding of persons through mutual debt. Source: Mauss, *The Gift* (1925) `verified`. Related: Blau (1964) `probable`.

**2. Plurality** — The condition of being with others in such a way that judgment is enlarged and action is political. Source: Arendt, *The Human Condition* (1958) `verified`. Related: Loidolt, *Phenomenology of Plurality* (2017) `probable`.

**3. Focal practice** — A practice that engages body, attention and community with a focal thing; the alternative to the device paradigm. Source: Borgmann, *Technology and the Character of Contemporary Life* (1984) `verified`.

**4. Resonance** — A relation to the world in which subjects are moved and respond; the opposite of alienation. Source: Rosa, *Resonance* (2019) `verified`. Related: Rosa, *Social Acceleration* (2013) `probable`.

**5. Friction** — The awkward, unequal, unstable and creative qualities of interconnection across difference; that which produces movement and effect. Source: Tsing, *Friction* (2005) `verified`.

**6. Aura** — The unique appearance of a distance, however close; the authenticity of the original that is lost in mechanical reproduction. Source: Benjamin, *Work of Art* (1935) `verified`.

**7. Task-orientation vs. time-orientation** — The distinction between working until the task is done and working for a wage measured by the clock. Source: Thompson, “Time, Work-Discipline” (1967) `verified`.

**8. Moral economy** — A pre-capitalist framework in which prices and wages are moral questions, not merely market outcomes. Source: Thompson, *Making of the English Working Class* (1963) `verified`.

**9. Filter bubble** — The personalised universe of information created by algorithmic filters, from which the user cannot see what is missing. Source: Pariser, *The Filter Bubble* (2011) `verified`. Contested by Bruns (2019) `verified`.

**10. Social capital** — The stock of reciprocal obligations, trust and civic engagement that enables collective action. Source: Putnam, *Bowling Alone* (2000) `verified`. Contested by critics who question the causal direction and the decline narrative.

**11. Attention economy** — The political economy in which attention is captured and commodified as a resource. Source: Bhandari, *Attention and Alienation* (2025) `probable`. Related: Lee, “How brain rot fuels the trillion-dollar AI economy” (2026) `probable`.

**12. Algorithmic drift** — The slow, often invisible reshaping of identity, taste and worldview by recommender systems. Source: “Algorithmic Drift” (2024) `probable`. Related: “Recommendation Drift” (2026) `probable`.

**13. Impersonal trust** — Trust based on indirect contact, formal relations and quantified knowledge, as opposed to personal trust based on direct contact and informal relations. Source: Wissenschaftskolleg zu Berlin `probable`. Related: Olegario, *A Culture of Credit* (2006) `verified`.
