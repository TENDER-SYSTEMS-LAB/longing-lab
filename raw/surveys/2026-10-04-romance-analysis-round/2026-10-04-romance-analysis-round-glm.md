**The recursion the table hides:** every wave mourned the previous wave's take. ## A.2 Wave notes (selected detail)

**1–2. Luddites and time discipline.** Hobsbawm read machine-breaking as collective bargaining by riot — a rational defence of customary wage norms, not technophobia `[V]`. Thompson's *Making* frames Luddism as a community defending not "jobs" but a whole way of owing and being owed — custom, apprenticeship, the family workshop `[V]`. Thompson's 1967 essay then shows the deeper taking: task-orientation ("n natural hours") replaced by clock-time; "Saint Monday" (the self-granted extension of the weekend) disciplined away `[V]`. In the author's terms: the first wave took *judgment and reflection as an aspect of work itself*, and the communal right to pace one's own time. Mumford had already argued the clock, not the steam engine, was the key machine of the industrial age `[V]`.

**3. Railways.** Schivelbusch documents how rail travel created "panoramic perception" — the landscape as commodity glimpsed — and how contemporaries experienced speed as the loss of the world between places `[V]`. The period phrase "the annihilation of space and time" is his organising evidence `[V]`. Carey's telegraph essay argues the deeper break: for the first time communication separated from transportation, making information tradable and markets national — the first abstraction of the message from the person carrying it `[V]`.

**4. Photography and sound.** Benjamin's aura is precisely the author's "origin": the here-and-now uniqueness of a thing or presence; mechanical reproduction detaches the image from origin and ritual `[V]`. Sontag: "to photograph is to appropriate the thing photographed" `[V]`. Barthes: the photograph as "flat death" — time without the living encounter `[V]`. Sterne shows sound reproduction trained "audile technique" — listening to recordings is a learned displacement of listening to persons `[V]`. Stiegler generalises: recorded media create "tertiary retention," memory deposited outside the person and the group, which forms consciousness but can also short-circuit its living transmission `[V]`.

**5. Telephone.** Fischer's careful empirical history found the telephone did not create new sociability; it intensified and re-arranged existing patterns (kin, women's networks) — a rare anti-determinist anchor in this literature `[V]`. This matters for the author's Point 5 test: the literature's baseline is that *effects depend on existing social structure*, a caution against attributing everything to the device.

**6. Automobile and suburbs.** Jacobs on the sidewalk ballet as the city's real parliament; cars make the street a corridor `[V]`. Oldenburg's third places — the café, barbershop, pub — as the host of regular, unprogrammed gathering; suburbia and the car dissolved their catchment `[V]`. Illich: the typical American male spent over 1,600 hours a year on his car (driving, earning for it, tending it) — a *balance* whose ledger the author's walk example inverts `[V]`. Putnam's rule of thumb: each additional ten minutes of daily commuting associated with roughly 10% less community involvement `[V — his claim; effect approximate]`. Ingold supplies the concept the author's walk needs: *wayfaring* — movement as knowing — versus *transport*, which is only destination `[V]`.

**7–8. Broadcast and consumerism.** Meyrowitz: electronic media merged information systems that separated child/adult, public/private — the backstage of everything exposed `[V]`. Postman: from the typographic mind (propositional, reflective) to the image mind (entertained) `[V]`. Gerbner's cultivation research: heavy viewing produces "mean world" perception `[V]`. Putnam's cohort evidence makes TV the prime suspect for 1960s–90s civic decline `[V]` — contested (see A.4). On consumer culture, Campbell's important complication: consumerism was *sold* through the romantic imagination — modern consumption is itself the historical product of romanticism `[V]`; Illouz shows the market's grammar eventually colonising love itself `[V]`.

**9–12. Computing to feeds.** The internet literature is the clearest case of contested-then-revised evidence (see A.4, box on the "internet paradox"). Ling's mobile-telephone work is subtle: the phone coordinates gatherings into being *and* corrodes presence — both `[V]`. boyd's ethnography gives the structural reason behind teen "addiction": physical public space (malls, streets) closed to adolescents by adult fear and scheduling; social media is the remaining commons `[V]`. The feed-era literature then converges on capture: Simon's attention scarcity `[V]`, Wu's attention merchants `[V]`, Schüll's machine zone (slot machines as the prehistory of engagement design) `[V]`, Zuboff's behavioral surplus `[V]`, Han's swarm that expels the Other while feeling warm `[V]`, Shiller's narrative epidemics as the monetisation of "light facts" `[V]`.

## A.3 How credit changed: from persons to systems

This is the author's Point 8 ground, and the history is rich.

**Personal credit as moral economy.** In medieval and early modern Europe most exchange ran on credit between neighbours; "credit" fused with credere — belief — and creditworthiness with character. Muldrew shows 17th-century England's economy was a web of mutual obligation in which litigation over debt was endemic and reputation was collateral `[V]`. Graeber's synthesis: credit ledgers among acquaintances precede coined money; coinage appears at the boundaries where strangers meet and trust fails `[V]`.

**Techniques of distance.** Trust was *stretched*, not replaced: bills of exchange and the "law merchant" at the Champagne fairs let merchants settle multilaterally without coin, enforced by community reputation (Milgrom, North & Weingast) `[P]`. Rotating credit associations — the Korean 계, Japanese mujin/tanomoshi, Chinese hui, Ghanaian susu, Mexican tanda — remain living hybrids of personal belief and pooled capital; Geertz's classic paper frames them as a "middle rung" between personal and institutional finance `[V]`. (Korean practices 두레 and 품앗이 — communal labour exchange — belong to the same family; the practices are well documented, but I cannot cite the specific scholarship from memory `[U — scholarship; V — practice]`.)

**Institutionalisation.** The Bank of England (1694) and the English national debt created impersonal, tradable belief (Dickson) `[P]`. In the 19th century, credit rating appeared: Lewis Tappan's Mercantile Agency (1841) — later R.G. Dun — turned a merchant's character into a file; Sandage shows failure was thereby reinterpreted from communal event to personal, scored identity `[P on details; V on existence]`. Consumer credit followed: installment plans (Singer, 1850s), auto loans (GMAC, 1919), charge cards (Diners Club, 1950), bureaus, and finally the FICO score (1989) — trust between persons replaced by a number between strangers `[P on years; V on sequence]`. Beito documents the parallel decline of fraternal mutual aid — sickness and death benefits owed person-to-person — displaced by insurance and the welfare state `[P]`.

**Securitisation.** In the late 20th century even the *institutional* layer was abstracted again: loans became models. MacKenzie's work on performativity (Black–Scholes; and his sociology-of-knowledge analysis of the 2008 credit crisis) shows the models did not merely describe markets — they shaped the belief they measured, and when the shared belief broke, the floor broke `[V; details P]`.

**What it did to trust between people.** Three findings, honestly stated:

- *Displacement.* Giddens: modernity "disembeds" social relations from local contexts and re-embeds them in abstract systems — money, expert systems — sustained by faceless commitments. Personal trust becomes *confidence in systems* `[V]`. Luhmann's sharper version: trust reduces complexity; much of what we call trust in modernity is mere *confidence* in functioning machinery, a different thing `[V]`.
- *Emancipation.* Impersonal credit freed people from the neighbour's opinion. For women, servants, minorities, and outsiders, "everyone knowing you" was surveillance, not warmth. This is the strongest counterweight to the author's nostalgia: the old floor was also a ceiling (see B.8).
- *Persistence.* Zelizer complicates the whole narrative: even inside institutional money, people earmark, domesticate, and relationalise — money never fully became impersonal in practice `[V]`.

In the author's terms: **belief** migrated from persons to systems; **understanding** of one's counterpart became a score; **trust** became confidence; and **default changed from social death to a fee**. That last sentence is my synthesis, not any single source's claim — and it is precisely the artwork's Point 8, with the literature's ambivalence attached.

## A.4 Contested-evidence boxes (both sides stated)

**TV and social capital.** *For decline:* Putnam's cohort and time-use evidence; Gerbner; Postman. *Against:* Ladd's re-survey of associational life `[P]`; Skocpol's finding that federations declined but advocacy groups rose — a change of *form*, not only a fall `[V]`; Fischer's work showing family/friend contact changed little 1974–2004 `[P on scope]`. Verdict: real decline in some measures (formal association, trust), contested attribution to TV specifically.

**Smartphones and youth wellbeing.** *For harm:* Twenge's cohort findings (in-person friend time, dating, driving, sleep all falling after ~2010) `[V]`; Haidt's synthesis arguing for a "great rewiring" around 2010–15, strongest for adolescent girls `[V]`. *Against:* Orben & Przybylski's specification-curve analyses finding tiny associations (well under 1% of variance; the famous comparison to mundane factors like eating potatoes circulated around this paper) `[V]`; Odgers & Jensen's review stressing cross-sectional design, small effects, and reverse causality `[P]`; Odgers's 2024 critique of Haidt `[P]`. Verdict: genuinely contested; the honest statement is "small average effects, possibly larger for specific groups (adolescent girls, heavy social-media use), causality unproven."

**Filter bubbles.** *For:* Pariser's thesis `[V]`; Sunstein's group-polarisation mechanism `[V]`. *Against:* Gentzkow & Shapiro find online ideological segregation modestly higher than offline, not exploding `[P on details]`; Dubois & Blank find most users have diverse diets, moderated by political interest `[P]`; Bruns's book-length critique `[V]`. Complicating both: Bail et al.'s experiment — *exposure to opposing views on Twitter increased polarization* `[V]` — the echo-chamber causal story fails in both directions. Verdict: contested; naive algorithmic-bubble claims unsupported, but affective polarisation is real and mechanisms are disputed.

**Cognitive offloading.** *Evidence:* Sparrow, Liu & Wegner's "Google effect" — people remember *where* information lives rather than the information itself when future access is expected `[V]`; Risko & Gilbert's framework paper on offloading as core cognition, adaptive in scope but capacity-costly `[P]`; Firth et al.'s review of internet-cognition effects `[P]`; GPS/hippocampus findings from Bohbot's lab (habitual GPS use associated with reduced spatial-memory engagement) `[P — design mostly correlational]`. *Against:* the long-term-harm extrapolations are not established; Carr's popular synthesis `[V]` is contested by cognitive scientists as overstated. Verdict: offloading is real and measurable; its romantic cost (reflection capacity) is a hypothesis, not a finding.

**The "internet paradox" flip.** Kraut et al. 1998 found more internet use → more loneliness and depression `[V]`; their own 2002 follow-up found the effects had largely dissipated, with benefits for some users `[V]`; Wellman's networked individualism reframed the question: the internet thins *groups* and thickens *networks* — a different social form, neither simply gathering nor scattering `[V]`. Lesson for the author: "scatter" needs a definition precise enough to survive this literature.

**Loneliness trends.** The 2023 US Surgeon General advisory declares an epidemic and cites steep declines in adolescent time with friends `[V — advisory exists; specific figures P]`; Cacioppo's loneliness research established it as a health risk factor `[P]`; Klinenberg shows solo living rising sharply without implying equal rises in loneliness `[V]`; Hertz's synthesis `[P]`. Verdict: partial — strong policy claims resting on mixed time-series.

# B. The author's eight points against published thought

Format per point: *supports* (source's claim → how it bears), *complicates*, *strongest objection*. My readings are separated from source claims by that structure.

## B.1 Origin: romantic = originating in one's own judgment; medium irrelevant

**Supports.** Benjamin's aura: the work's authority lies in its here-and-now origin; reproduction detaches image from origin and ritual — the author's Point 1 in aesthetic form `[V]`. Charles Taylor's authenticity: the modern ideal that each life and judgment be one's own; its corruption is the author's target `[V]`. Trilling's sincerity/authenticity pair gives the concept its history `[V]`. Kant's aesthetics: a judgment of taste must be one's own, disinterested, non-conceptual — "origin in judgment" has a canonical philosophical anchor `[V]`.

**Complicates.** Habermas: the *private interiority* the author treats as medium-independent was itself constituted by a medium — the letter- and novel-reading family of the 18th century. The "own judgment" self is a product of print culture, so "the medium does not matter" is historically false in the relevant sense `[V]`. Adorno's *Jargon of Authenticity*: authenticity-talk is itself an ideology that hides social conditions `[V]`. Barthes's death of the author: meaning's origin is distributed, not singular `[P on venue]`. Wegner's transactive memory: groups literally share cognition — knowing *who* knows is part of knowing; judgment was never solo `[V]`. Zuboff's "anticipatory conformity": systems shape behaviour before it is declared, so people may sincerely believe a judgment is their own when its seed was engineered `[V]`.

**Strongest objection.** The test is unfalsifiable and applied retrospectively: no one can verify in the moment that a judgment's origin is unmediated, and the literature shows both that interiority is medium-constituted (Habermas) and that it is socially distributed (Wegner). A careful critic says Point 1 enshrines the habits of one media-historical moment (letters, long walks) as an ontology — the very move Williams and Boym diagnose as golden-age recursion.

## B.2 Exchange as mutual bonds: love, understanding, trust arise by owing

**Supports.** Mauss: the gift is a "total prestation" — to give, to receive, to reciprocate are obligatory; bonds *are* the debt `[V]`. Gouldner's norm of reciprocity as a universal social norm `[V]`. Sahlins's reciprocity continuum (generalised/balanced/negative) maps kinship to hostility `[V]`. Graeber: sociality is debt-ledgers all the way down; "baseline communism" underlies all exchange `[V]`. Zelizer: even monetary payments perform relational work — payment *is* bond-making `[V]`.

**Complicates.** Derrida, *Given Time*: a gift that is recognised as a gift — entered into any ledger, even gratitude's — is annulled as gift and becomes exchange; the purest bond would be the one *not* bookkept `[P on years]`. Levinas: responsibility to the Other is asymmetric and infinite, not balanced — the author's balance metaphor is ethically the opposite of Levinas's face `[V]`. Buber: I–Thou is encounter, not transaction; the It-ledger is precisely what love escapes `[V]`. Mauss's own evidence cuts both ways: hierarchical gifts bind and humiliate (potlatch, kula) — owing can dominate as well as unite `[V]`.

**Strongest objection.** The ledger metaphor imports the market form into intimacy — exactly the "cold intimacy" Illouz diagnoses as modern love's disease `[V]`. A careful critic argues the author is right that bonds require mutual owing, but wrong to model it as *balances*: the literature's better model is asymmetric, non-identical, remembered-but-not-tallied obligation.

## B.3 Balances decrease via light facts and rumours; the problem is erosion, not hatred's rise

**Supports.** Han's *In the Swarm*: digital mass media produce not classical negativity (hate's otherness) but a smooth positivity — the like, the swarm, the absence of the Other; erosion without opposition is Han's explicit thesis `[P on year]`. Durkheim's anomie is erosion of regulation and bond, not aggression — the concept matches the author's asymmetry `[V]`. Sunstein's *On Rumors*: rumours destroy trust by mechanism, not by malice `[V]`. Shiller's narrative economics: belief moves in contagious epidemics of "light" stories — a market-theory formalisation of the author's claim `[V]`. Habermas's public-sphere degradation ("refeudalisation") is likewise erosion of the space where understanding forms `[V]`.

**Complicates.** Empirically, measured hostility *has* risen on the author's target platforms: Bail et al.'s experiment shows exposure producing more polarised hostility `[V]`; Phillips's ethnography of trolling shows organised cruelty `[P]`. The decrease/increase dichotomy may be false: erosion and enmity co-produce. Gossip, the original "light fact," is for Dunbar the *foundational* social technology — a counterexample to treating light facts as purely corrosive `[V]`. And declining trust in institutions may track real failures (inequality, scandals), not media effects — Putnam & Garrett blame the mid-century turn on economics and politics, not only media `[V]`.

**Strongest objection.** The four balances are treated as measurable quantities without a measurement theory. Trust data are item-specific and trend-mixed (institutional trust down; interpersonal items flatter — see Fischer `[P]`); "belief" and "understanding" have no agreed metric at all. The critic's line: as stated, Point 3 is a metaphor looking for an instrument — which is acceptable in an artwork, but must not be mistaken for an empirical claim.

## B.4 What is human: doing, and doing with; mistakes and inefficiency are romantic

**Supports.** Borgmann's device paradigm: the stove *gathers*; the central furnace delivers warmth while concealing the machinery and dispersing the household; "focal practices" (running, bread-baking, tending) restore engagement — the author's Point 4 in sociology's most precise form `[V]`. Illich: beyond a critical speed, conviviality collapses; time freed by speed is consumed by serving the speed `[V]`. Ingold's wayfaring vs. transport names the forty-minute walk exactly `[V]`. Tanizaki: electric light's brightness took the shadows in which beauty lived — an aesthetic case against efficiency `[V]`. Zhuangzi: the gardener who refuses the well-sweep — "where there are machines, there are machine hearts" (機械 → 機心); the earliest formulation of the author's claim `[P on chapter]`. Sennett's craftsman and M. Polanyi's tacit knowledge: human competence lives in friction and unformalisable skill `[V]`. Rosa: resonance *requires* that the world resist — frictionless relation is alienation `[V]`. Inzlicht et al.'s "effort paradox": humans sometimes value effort intrinsically `[P]`. Bjork's "desirable difficulties": retention grows with friction `[P]`.

**Complicates.** Chosen friction is a luxury position: the inefficiency the author romanticises was historically borne by women, servants, and the poor, and Illich's own analysis shows speed-inequality maps onto wealth `[V]`. Modernization also took *drudgery*: water-carrying, child mortality, factory maiming. The claim "efficiency is not romance" proves too much — efficient mercy is still mercy; the洗碗 machine freed hours that someone (historically: women) spent, or should have spent, with persons. Hochschild's *Time Bind* shows the freed hours went to work, not to persons — the taking was in the allocation, not only the appliance `[V]`.

**Strongest objection.** A careful critic splits the concept: *imposed* friction is oppression; *chosen* friction is romance; modernization's crime was converting the first into commodities while selling the second as convenience. The author's Point 4 conflates the two, and without the split, the artwork risks aestheticising poverty — Sontag's and Boym's standing warning against nostalgia for other people's hardship `[V]`.

## B.5 The test for AI: gather or scatter; grow reflection or replace it

**Supports.** Turkle's work is the literature's version of the test: does the technology substitute for conversation (robot pets, "alone together") or occasion it? `[V]`. Illich's conviviality gives the criterion a pre-AI form: tools under human purpose vs. tools that dictate ends `[V]`. Engelbart's "Augmenting Human Intellect" and Licklider's "Man-Computer Symbiosis" are the founding documents of the *augmentation* tradition — AI designed explicitly to grow reflection `[V]`. Fishkin's deliberative polling shows engineered gathering measurably improves judgment `[V]`.

**Complicates.** The time-save assumption fails historically: Keynes's 1930 prediction of a 15-hour week; the hours were reallocated to consumption and work instead (Schor `[V]`; Skidelsky & Skidelsky `[P]`; Hochschild's time bind `[V]`; Aguiar & Hurst show leisure rose for some groups but flowed to screens `[P]`). Wellman's networked individualism warns that "scatter" is the wrong picture: people are hyper-connected into *networks* while groups thin — neither gathered nor scattered `[V]`. And scattering long predates AI (Points 6–11 above), so the test risks scapegoating the newest wave for an older trend — Winner's and the SCOT literature's standing point that artifacts embody politics and that effects are socially shaped `[V]`.

**Strongest objection.** The test is applied to a variable (AI) whose effect the literature says is *contingent on social structure* (Fischer's telephone; Wellman's networks). A careful critic: "gather or scatter" is not a property of the tool but of the institutions around it; the artwork's model, which attributes the decline to the technology's structure, will mistime and misattribute — the decline began with the automobile and the television, and AI inherits the trajectory rather than causing it.

## B.6 Drift through comfort: neither robbed nor freely given

**Supports.** The Odyssey itself supplies the canon — and the Lotus-eaters (Book 9) are the *closer* sibling to the author's Circe: they "forgot their homecoming," losing not just desire but the *knowledge of what was lost* — precisely Point 6's endpoint. Hermes' moly is the countermeasure: preserved memory against comfort. Schüll's machine zone: engineered absorption, the "ludic loop," slot machines as the prehistory of infinite-scroll design `[V]`. Zuboff: extraction dressed as service — closer to *robbery* than the author allows `[V]`. Wu: the attention merchant's business model is engineered drift `[V]`. Han: comfort's power expels the Other without pain `[P]`. Stiegler: proletarianisation — the loss of savoir-faire and savoir-vivre to industrial memory systems; people no longer know that they no longer know `[P on volume]`. Variable-ratio reinforcement, formalised by Skinner, is the mechanism Schüll and the engagement literature both invoke `[V as concept]`.

**Complicates.** Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan): media satisfy real psychological needs (competence, relatedness, autonomy-simulacra) — "drift" pathologises need-meeting `[P on specific papers]`. Cohen's moral-panic framework: every wave's comfort-scared its elders — novels, radio, comics, TV `[V]`. And the middle position has critics on both flanks: Zuboff says it is robbery; the choice theorists say it is choice.

**Strongest objection.** "Neither robbed nor freely given" understates *active design*: dark patterns, engagement-optimised ranking, and the documented internal research on harms (Haugen's 2021 disclosures) are intentional architectures, not drift `[P on details]`. A careful critic: the Circe image exonerates the sorceress; the literature on the attention economy is a literature about sorcery with a business plan. At minimum the ontology needs a variable for *engineered* vs. *ambient* comfort.

## B.7 Gathering made people human; its decline tracks each-of-us-alone-with-machines

**Supports.** Arendt: action and speech among others is where humanity discloses itself; plurality is *the* human condition; the space of appearance is made by gathering `[V]`. Durkheim's collective effervescence and Turner's communitas: ritual co-presence generates the social bond itself `[V]`. Collins's interaction ritual chains: emotional energy and symbols require bodily co-presence and mutual focus — the strongest empirical-anthropological support for "gathering made people human" `[V]`. Dewey: democracy as "conjoint communicated experience" `[V]`. Confucian canon: 《論語》"德不孤，必有鄰" (virtue is not solitary — it has neighbours) `[P on numbering]`; 《禮記·學記》"獨學而無友，則孤陋而寡聞" (learning alone, without friends, leaves one narrow and unheard) — the gathering-of-two as condition of knowing `[P]`. Ubuntu: a person is a person through other persons (Ramose) `[P]`. Korean 두레/품앗이 as lived institutions of shared labour `[V as practice]`.

**Complicates.** Gathering was always partial: Fraser's subaltern counterpublics — the canonical polis rested on slaves; the bourgeois public sphere on property-owning men `[V]`. The decline of gathering is misdated if attached to AI: Putnam's data begin its fall around the 1960s `[V]`; Klinenberg documents solo living rising for decades `[V]`. boyd shows young people *still* gather — online, because physical publics are closed `[V]`. And the author's own Point 1 (solitary reflection is romantic) sits in tension with Point 7 (gathering made people human); the author's ranking ("shared is more") is asserted, not derived.

**Strongest objection.** "Gathering made people human" is contradicted by the traditions the author elsewhere honours — the hermit, the mystic, Zhuangzi's solitary gardener, the walker thinking alone for forty minutes. A careful critic: the eight points oscillate between valuing solitude-as-origin and gathering-as-humanity; without a criterion for when each is romantic, the ontology will double-count or contradict itself.

## B.8 The market's floor is mutual belief and credit

**Supports.** Graeber: credit precedes coin; markets stand on ledgers of trust `[V]`. Polanyi: the self-regulating market was disembedded from a social fabric it then consumed — the "floor" is the social relation `[V]`. Keynes: markets move on animal spirits and newspaper beauty-contest beliefs — conventional judgment, not fundamentals `[V]`. Akerlof & Shiller: confidence and stories as macro variables `[V]`. Shiller: narrative epidemics literally drive prices `[V]`. MacKenzie: financial models are "an engine, not a camera" — they perform the reality they claim to measure, i.e., shared belief is load-bearing `[V]`. Beckert: under fundamental uncertainty, economic action rests on "fictional expectations" — belief as the operating system of capitalism `[V]`. Simmel: money is "pure interaction," sustained by a trust that outruns evidence `[V]`.

**Complicates.** Knapp's chartalism and the Weberian state: money's floor is also *taxation and law* — belief backed by force `[P on edition details]`. Transaction-cost economics (Coase; Williamson): institutions are built precisely to *minimise* the trust needed — collateral, clearing, law — so the floor is engineered distrust as much as belief `[V as tradition]`. And the emancipation argument (B.3, A.3): impersonal credit freed strangers — including the socially distrusted — to cooperate; Giddens's disembedding is enabling, not only loss-making `[V]`.

**Strongest objection.** The floor metaphor conflates two floors: *origin* (yes, mutual belief — Graeber, Beckert) and *operation* (no, a lattice of engineered distrust — law, scores, collateral). The artwork prices declines in belief; the literature says the modern system is precisely the machine that keeps running when belief between persons has already collapsed. A careful critic: Point 8 may be historically right about the floor's origin and wrong about its current function — and the difference matters for what the securities are meant to track.

## B.9 What the literature contains that the eight points lack

1. **Power, class, gender, race.** The eight points are remarkably classless. Hochschild's emotional labour `[V]` and second shift `[V]`; Hayden on suburban design's gendered costs `[P]`; Rothstein on how suburbs were racially engineered `[V]`; Fraser on who was excluded from "the public" `[V]`. *Whose* romance, preserved by *whose* friction, is unnamed.
2. **Chosen vs. imposed friction.** The split that rescues Point 4 (B.4) needs to be in the ontology.
3. **Embodiment and tacit knowledge.** M. Polanyi ("we know more than we can tell") `[V]`, Sennett `[V]`, Collins's co-presence requirement `[V]` — a category of the human distinct from "judgment and reflection."
4. **Rhythm, calendar, ritual time.** Durkheim `[V]`, Turner `[V]`, Han's rituals as communal time-knots `[P]`, Pieper's leisure-as-festival `[P]`. Gathering is an event; rhythm is a *pattern* — the author has the first, not the second.
5. **The material and ecological substrate.** Crawford's *Atlas of AI*: every "taking" has a mine and a warehouse attached; the romance of elsewhere is also destroyed `[V]`. Rosa's resonance-with-nature `[V]`.
6. **Institutions as carriers of loss.** Winner ("Do Artifacts Have Politics?") `[V]`; Bijker, Hughes & Pinch `[V]`. Zoning, highway acts, and redlining took gathering as much as any device; a top-down model that attributes decline to technologies will misprice institutional losses.
7. **Memory transmission and generational loss.** Assmann's cultural memory `[P]`; Stiegler's tertiary retention and proletarianisation — the specific loss where people *no longer know they do not know* `[P]`. The author's "drift" endpoint needs this mechanism.
8. **Attention as ethical act.** Weil — attention as prayer and "the rarest and purest form of generosity" `[P on attribution]`; Murdoch's "just and loving gaze" `[V]`. Distinct from reflection; arguably the *condition* of the origin-judgment.
9. **Play.** Huizinga: play is voluntary, unproductive, ordered — culture itself is played before it is thought `[V]`. Neither reflection nor efficiency; the eight points have no slot for it.
10. **Boredom and patience.** Benjamin's "The Storyteller": Erfahrung — shareable, slow experience — dies with information culture; boredom is the "dream bird" that hatches experience `[V]`. The precondition of Point 1's reflection.
11. **Weak ties and bridging capital.** Granovetter's weak ties `[V]`; Putnam's bridging vs. bonding `[V]`. The author's bonds are all bonding; the literature says modernization also dissolved *bridging* — the acquaintance, the passer-by — a different loss with different securities.
12. **Nostalgia reflexivity and the WEIRD problem.** Boym's restorative vs. reflective nostalgia `[V]`; Williams's golden-age recursion `[V]`; Henrich et al. on how unrepresentative the usual evidence base is `[V]`; and the non-Western balances (Zhuangzi `[P]`, Tanizaki `[V]`, the Confucian/Liji lines `[P]`, Ubuntu `[P]`, 계/두레/품앗이). An artwork that prices "loss" must know it is itself a nostalgia machine — Boym's *reflective* nostalgia (loving the ruin, not rebuilding it) is the mode the artwork can consciously occupy.

## Closing note on limits

1. **What this round cannot settle:** the causal weight of any single wave (the internet literature's flip shows why), and the measurement of the four balances (Point 3 has no instrument in the literature; the closest formal analogues are Shiller's narrative epidemiology and Beckert's fictional expectations).
2. **Most load-bearing verified sources for the author's eight points:** Borgmann (4, 6), Mauss/Graeber (2, 8), Collins/Arendt (7), Benjamin (1), Giddens/Luhmann (8, credit), Han/Zuboff (3, 6), Turkle (5).
3. **Biggest known gaps:** non-Western sources beyond the classical Chinese and Korean items; economic history of non-Atlantic credit systems; anything post-2020 (out of scope here by the brief).
4. **Standing recommendation:** every `[P]` and every **†** identifier must be library-verified before any downstream round treats it as fact.
