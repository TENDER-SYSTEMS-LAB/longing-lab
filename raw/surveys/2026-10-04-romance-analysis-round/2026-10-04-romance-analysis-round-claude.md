## Read-me: status marks and limits

Every source below is marked from my own knowledge; none was re-checked against a live catalogue in this round. Treat the marks as a confidence report, not a library verification.

- **verified** — I am confident of author, title, year and the claim attributed. Years are first publication in the original language; English translation years are given where they differ.
- **probable** — I believe the work exists and says roughly this, but a detail (year, journal, subtitle, exact finding) may be off.
- **unverified** — I recall it but cannot confirm it. Check before any use.

Two conventions. First, "Claim" lines say what the work argues; "Reading" lines are my interpretation of how it bears on the author's view. Second, the brief's wave list is kept, with one wave added (online dating, 1995–2020) because it bears directly on romance and has unusually good evidence.

Nothing here selects securities, proposes prices, or judges whether romance is disappearing. Where a section discusses "decline", it reports what sources claim.

Highest-risk citations for a check before use: anything marked probable or unverified, all post-2023 AI-and-cognition studies (fast-moving, several are preprints), and exact page or line references, which I have deliberately avoided.

## A1. Waves at a glance

Across thirteen waves the recurring loss is not contact itself but **friction-bearing, person-originated exchange**: each wave lowered the cost of a good and, with it, the occasions on which people had to judge, wait, gather or owe. The evidence is strongest for television, suburbs and social media, and most contested for the telephone, the early internet and smartphones.

## A2. Wave by wave

Each wave gives the strongest claim of loss, then the strongest complication. Contested empirical questions get both sides.

### 1. Mechanised work and the Luddites (1811–1816)

**Claim.** E. P. Thompson, *The Making of the English Working Class* (1963, verified), reads the Luddites as defending a customary "moral economy" — wages, standards and apprenticeship regulated by obligation — not as machine-haters. Hobsbawm, "The Machine Breakers" (*Past & Present* 1, 1952, verified), calls frame-breaking "collective bargaining by riot". Brian Merchant, *Blood in the Machine* (2023, verified), retells the story as a fight over who controls technology's gains.

**Reading.** What was taken was the worker's judgment over the work and the obligations binding masters and workers — the author's points 1 and 2 at the scale of a trade. The Luddites targeted machinery they judged "hurtful to commonality" (a phrase from Luddite letters, probable), which is close to the author's test in point 5.

**Complication.** Living standards for most workers rose over the following century; historians still argue the "standard of living debate" (Hobsbawm vs Hartwell, 1960s, probable). The loss was real for the generation displaced; whether it was a net loss is not settled.

### 2. Industrial time discipline (1770s–1900)

**Claim.** Thompson, "Time, Work-Discipline, and Industrial Capitalism" (*Past & Present* 38, 1967, verified), argues that factory work replaced task-orientation (work set by the job, interleaved with talk and rest) with clock-time, enforced by bells, fines, schools and sermons. Douglas Reid, "The Decline of Saint Monday 1766–1876" (*Past & Present* 71, 1976, probable), traces the loss of the informal Monday holiday. Lewis Mumford, *Technics and Civilization* (1934, verified), calls the clock, not the steam engine, the key machine of the industrial age.

**Reading.** This is the origin of the author's point 4: inefficiency stopped being a way of living and became a moral failing. The forty-minute walk is, in Thompson's terms, a return to task-time.

**Complication.** Glennie and Thrift, *Shaping the Day* (2009, probable), show that clock-time use was widespread in England well before factories, weakening the claim that industry imposed it. Pre-industrial "leisure" was also often underemployment and poverty.

### 3. Railways and the telegraph (1830s–1880s)

**Claim.** Wolfgang Schivelbusch, *The Railway Journey* (1977; English 1979, verified), argues rail travel "annihilated" the space between points and produced "panoramic" perception: landscape seen as a passing picture rather than travelled through. James Carey, "Technology and Ideology: The Case of the Telegraph" (1983; in *Communication as Culture*, 1989, verified), argues the telegraph separated communication from transportation, and turned news into a commodity of fact detached from place. Stephen Kern, *The Culture of Time and Space 1880–1918* (1983, verified), covers standard time (US railways 1883; Prime Meridian Conference 1884, verified). Thoreau in *Walden* (1854, verified) doubts that Maine and Texas, newly linked by telegraph, have anything important to say to each other.

**Reading.** Carey's separation of message from messenger is an early form of the author's point 1: content whose origin is no longer a person present. Thoreau anticipates point 3 — a flood of light facts.

**Complication.** Tom Standage, *The Victorian Internet* (1998, verified), shows the telegraph also hosted romance, gossip and courtship between operators. New media repeatedly carry the old intimacy.

### 4. Photography and recorded sound (1839–1930s)

**Claim.** Walter Benjamin, "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" (1935–36, verified), argues reproduction withers the "aura": a work's unique presence in time and place. Susan Sontag, *On Photography* (1977, verified), argues photographs substitute for experience and memory. John Philip Sousa, "The Menace of Mechanical Music" (*Appleton's Magazine*, 1906, probable), warned that the phonograph would end amateur singing and parlour music. Jonathan Sterne, *The Audible Past* (2003, verified), gives the cultural history of sound reproduction.

**Reading.** Benjamin's aura maps closely onto the author's point 1: value comes from origin, not content. Sousa names a lost **gathering** — families making music together — replaced by listening.

**Complication.** Benjamin himself saw emancipatory potential in reproduction. Roland Barthes, *Camera Lucida* (1980, verified), finds in photographs a piercing personal "punctum" — a new form of reflection rather than its loss.

### 5. The telephone (1876–1950s)

**Claim.** Claude Fischer, *America Calling: A Social History of the Telephone to 1940* (1992, verified), finds that the telephone was adopted largely for sociability (driven by women, against the industry's business-use marketing) and that it tended to reinforce local ties rather than dissolve them. Carolyn Marvin, *When Old Technologies Were New* (1988, verified), shows 19th-century anxieties about electric media — including fears about courtship and class mixing. Ithiel de Sola Pool, ed., *The Social Impact of the Telephone* (1977, verified).

**Reading.** The telephone is the clearest pre-AI counter-case: a technology that increased exchange between persons whose origin was their own voice. Under the author's test (point 5) it would score as gathering.

**Complication on the other side.** The composed letter — slow, revised, kept — lost ground. The telephone moved exchange from reflection to immediacy.

### 6. The automobile and the suburbs (1910s–1970s)

**Claim.** Kenneth Jackson, *Crabgrass Frontier* (1985, verified), traces American suburbanisation and the privatisation of life. Jane Jacobs, *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* (1961, verified), defends sidewalk life, mixed use and "eyes on the street". Ray Oldenburg, *The Great Good Place* (1989, verified), names "third places" — cafés, bars, barbershops — and their decline. Putnam, *Bowling Alone* (2000, verified), estimates that each additional ten minutes of commuting cuts civic involvement by about ten percent.

**Reading.** The car removed **chance encounter** and **walking time** — the author's forty-minute walk becomes structurally impossible when distances are built for cars.

**Complication.** Herbert Gans, *The Levittowners* (1967, verified), found early suburbanites sociable and active. Putnam treats sprawl as a minor factor (he attributes a modest share of decline to it, probable on the exact share).

### 7. Radio and television (1920s–1990s)

**Claim.** Putnam (1995 article, verified; 2000 book, verified) names generational change and television as the main drivers of declining American civic engagement. Neil Postman, *Amusing Ourselves to Death* (1985, verified), argues television turned public discourse into entertainment, displacing print-based reasoning. Joshua Meyrowitz, *No Sense of Place* (1985, verified), argues electronic media dissolved the link between physical place and social situation.

**Empirical evidence, both sides.** Benjamin Olken, "Do Television and Radio Destroy Social Capital? Evidence from Indonesian Villages" (*AEJ: Applied* 2009, verified), uses signal variation and finds more TV and radio reception associated with less participation in community groups and lower self-reported trust. Matthew Gentzkow, "Television and Voter Turnout" (*QJE* 2006, verified), finds TV's introduction reduced turnout. Against Putnam: Pippa Norris, "Does Television Erode Social Capital? A Reply to Putnam" (*PS* 1996, probable), finds the effect depends on content (news viewers were more engaged); Michael Schudson, "What If Civic Life Didn't Die?" (*American Prospect* 1996, probable), argues engagement changed form rather than vanished. Lynn Spigel, *Make Room for TV* (1992, verified), shows television was also designed and sold as a family gathering point.

**Reading.** Television is the strongest evidence for the author's point 7 before the internet, and the case where the causal evidence is best.

### 8. Mass consumer culture (1920s–1970s)

**Claim.** Tibor Scitovsky, *The Joyless Economy* (1976, verified), distinguishes **comfort** (relief from discomfort) from **pleasure/stimulation** (novelty, skill, challenge), and argues affluent economies over-supply comfort, producing boredom. Herbert Marcuse, *One-Dimensional Man* (1964, verified), opens by describing a comfortable, smooth, democratic unfreedom in advanced industrial society. Jean Baudrillard, *The Consumer Society* (1970, verified). Stuart Ewen, *Captains of Consciousness* (1976, verified), on advertising's shaping of desire.

**Reading.** Scitovsky and Marcuse are the strongest academic sources for the author's point 6 (drift through comfort) — written fifty years before feeds.

**Complication.** Consumer goods also relieved real drudgery, especially women's. But Ruth Schwartz Cowan, *More Work for Mother* (1983, verified), shows household technology did not reduce housework time; standards rose instead.

### 9. The personal computer and the internet (1980s–2000s)

**Empirical evidence, both sides.** Kraut et al., "Internet Paradox" (*American Psychologist* 1998, verified), found greater internet use associated with small declines in family communication and increases in loneliness and depression. Kraut et al., "Internet Paradox Revisited" (*Journal of Social Issues* 2002, verified), found those negative effects had largely disappeared in follow-up. Howard Rheingold, *The Virtual Community* (1993, verified), describes real online communities. Barry Wellman and collaborators ("Physical Place and Cyberplace", 2001, probable; Rainie & Wellman, *Networked*, 2012, verified) describe "networked individualism": ties persist but are organised around the individual rather than the group.

**Theory.** Hubert Dreyfus, *On the Internet* (2001, verified), argues that disembodied interaction lacks risk and commitment, and so cannot build skill or meaning. Sherry Turkle, *Life on the Screen* (1995, verified), was largely optimistic at this stage.

**Reading.** Wellman's networked individualism is the exact concept for the author's "scatter" in point 5: ties do not vanish, but gathering is replaced by one-to-one switchboards.

### 10. Mobile phones (1990s–2007)

**Claim.** Rich Ling, *The Mobile Connection* (2004, verified), documents "micro-coordination" — replacing fixed plans with running adjustment. Ling, *New Tech, New Ties* (2008, verified), uses Randall Collins's interaction rituals to argue mobiles strengthen small intimate circles while weakening wider co-present groups. Kenneth Gergen, "The Challenge of Absent Presence" (in Katz & Aakhus, eds., *Perpetual Contact*, 2002, probable), names being physically present but mentally elsewhere.

**Contested.** Przybylski and Weinstein (*J. Social and Personal Relationships* 2013, probable) reported that the mere presence of a phone lowered closeness in conversation; later replication attempts have been mixed or null (probable).

**Reading.** Micro-coordination removes the **promise** — the plan made in advance and kept — which is a small bond in the author's sense (point 2).

### 11. Online dating (1995–2020)

**Claim.** Rosenfeld, Thomas and Hausen, "Disintermediating Your Friends" (*PNAS* 2019, verified), show that meeting online became the most common way US heterosexual couples met by 2017, displacing meeting through friends and family. Eva Illouz, *Cold Intimacies* (2007, verified), argues online dating turns self and partner into catalogued profiles, rationalising romance. Alain Badiou, *In Praise of Love* (2009, verified), criticises "risk-free" love sold by dating sites.

**Reading.** "Disintermediation" names a loss in the author's terms precisely: friends who vouched for a match were extending credit (point 2) and gathering (point 7). Dating apps replace the network's credit with a platform's ranking.

**Complication.** Rosenfeld and colleagues find online couples are not less stable; online meeting helped same-sex couples and others with thin local markets.

### 12. Social media and smartphones (2004–2020)

**Claim.** Sherry Turkle, *Alone Together* (2011, verified) and *Reclaiming Conversation* (2015, verified), argues that connection is displacing conversation and solitude. Jean Twenge, *iGen* (2017, verified), and Jonathan Haidt, *The Anxious Generation* (2024, verified), argue that the shift to phone-based childhood around 2010–2015 caused rising adolescent anxiety and depression.

**Empirical evidence, both sides.**

- Supporting harm: Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer and Gentzkow, "The Welfare Effects of Social Media" (*AER* 2020, verified) — deactivating Facebook for four weeks modestly raised wellbeing, reduced political polarisation and increased offline time with friends and family. Braghieri, Levy and Makarin, "Social Media and Mental Health" (*AER* 2022, verified) — Facebook's staggered college rollout worsened student mental health.
- Against strong harm: Orben and Przybylski, "The association between adolescent well-being and digital technology use" (*Nature Human Behaviour* 2019, verified) — associations are real but very small, comparable to eating potatoes. Candice Odgers's review of Haidt (*Nature* 2024, probable) argues the causal case is not established. Amy Orben, "The Sisyphean Cycle of Technology Panics" (*Perspectives on Psychological Science* 2020, verified), shows recurring panics over each new medium.
- Time with friends: US time-use data show a sharp fall in in-person socialising, especially among young people, from the 2000s; the US Surgeon General's advisory *Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation* (2023, verified) summarises these trends. Whether phones caused the fall is contested.

**Reading.** The time-use decline in face-to-face socialising is the most robust finding and bears on point 7; the mental-health causal story is the most contested and should not carry weight in the model alone.

### 13. Algorithmic feeds (2009–2020)

**Claim.** Eli Pariser, *The Filter Bubble* (2011, verified), argues personalisation narrows exposure. Shoshana Zuboff, *The Age of Surveillance Capitalism* (2019, verified), argues platforms extract behavioural data to predict and modify behaviour. Tim Wu, *The Attention Merchants* (2016, verified). Taina Bucher, *If…Then* (2018, verified), and Nick Seaver, *Computing Taste* (2022, verified), study how algorithms shape experience.

**Empirical evidence, both sides.** Against strong filter bubbles: Bakshy, Messing and Adamic (*Science* 2015, verified) found users' own choices limited cross-cutting exposure more than the ranking did; Flaxman, Goel and Rao (*Public Opinion Quarterly* 2016, verified) found modest segregation; Axel Bruns, *Are Filter Bubbles Real?* (2019, verified), argues no. The 2020 US election studies with Meta — Guess et al. (*Science* 2023, probable) found switching to a chronological feed reduced time on platform but did not measurably change polarisation; Nyhan et al. (*Nature* 2023, probable) similar. For amplification effects: Huszár et al. (*PNAS* 2022, verified) found Twitter's algorithm amplified political content, more for the right in most countries studied.

**Reading.** The feeds literature supports the author's point 6 better than point 3. The evidence for narrowing is weak; the evidence that feeds capture **attention and time** — and so displace gathering and reflection — is stronger. The loss is less "we see only our side" than "we no longer choose what to look at".

## A3. How credit itself changed

Credit moved from **reputation known to persons** to **scores computed about persons**, roughly 1700–1990; trust between people was not abolished but was increasingly replaced by trust in systems. The literature agrees on the direction and disagrees on whether this destroyed interpersonal trust or simply freed it from economic duty.

| Stage | Approx. dates | How creditworthiness was known | Key sources (status) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Personal credit webs | 1500s–1700s (England) | Neighbourhood reputation; most transactions on informal credit; "credit" meant both debt and good name | Muldrew, *The Economy of Obligation* (1998, verified) |
| Notaries and brokers | 1600s–1800s (France) | Intermediaries who knew both parties matched lenders and borrowers | Hoffman, Postel-Vinay & Rosenthal, *Priceless Markets* (2000, verified); *Dark Matter Credit* (2019, verified) |
| Merchant coalitions | Medieval Mediterranean | Group reputation and collective punishment | Greif, "Contract Enforceability… Maghribi Traders" (*AER* 1993, verified) |
| Rotating credit associations | Persisting worldwide | Members pool and take turns; trust enforced by membership | Geertz, "The Rotating Credit Association" (1962, probable); Korean *gye* (계) as a case, sources unverified here |
| Credit reporting agencies | 1841 onward (US) | Written reports on character by local correspondents | Mercantile Agency, founded by Lewis Tappan 1841 (verified); Olegario, *A Culture of Credit* (2006, verified); Lauer, *Creditworthy* (2017, verified) |
| Consumer instalment credit | 1910s–1950s | Retailers' and finance companies' files | Calder, *Financing the American Dream* (1999, verified); Hyman, *Debtor Nation* (2011, verified) |
| Bank cards and scoring | 1958 (BankAmericard) – 1989 (FICO score) | Statistical scores replace character judgments | Poon, "From New Deal institutions to capital markets" (*Accounting, Organizations and Society* 2009, probable); Fourcade & Healy, "Classification situations" (*AOS* 2013, verified) |

**Claims.** Craig Muldrew argues early modern English society ran on chains of informal credit, so that economic life was a moral web of trust; "credit" was a social judgment of a person. Josh Lauer shows that 19th-century credit reporting first tried to record **character** — habits, family, morals — and later replaced it with financial data and scores, partly because scores looked objective and non-discriminatory. Fourcade and Healy argue scoring creates new "classification situations" that sort people by data-derived rank. Viviana Zelizer, *The Social Meaning of Money* (1994, verified), shows people keep earmarking money socially even in impersonal markets.

**Theory of the shift.** Georg Simmel, *The Philosophy of Money* (1900, verified), argues money enables freedom and impersonality: dependence spreads across many anonymous people instead of a few known ones. Anthony Giddens, *The Consequences of Modernity* (1990, verified), calls this **disembedding**: social relations lifted out of local contexts into "abstract systems" (money, expert systems), sustained by faceless trust. Niklas Luhmann, *Trust and Power* (German 1968/1973; English 1979, verified), distinguishes personal trust from system trust. Lynne Zucker, "Production of Trust" (*Research in Organizational Behavior* 1986, probable), shows US institutional trust (regulation, credentials) replacing process-based trust around 1840–1920. Susan Shapiro, "The Social Control of Impersonal Trust" (*AJS* 1987, verified). Karl Polanyi, *The Great Transformation* (1944, verified), frames the whole shift as the market "disembedding" from society.

**Counter-view.** David Graeber, *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011, verified), argues that credit long preceded coinage and that impersonal, enforceable debt tends to be backed by violence and to dissolve human relations into calculation. Laurence Fontaine, *The Moral Economy* (2014, verified), shows pre-modern credit was also a tool of domination, dependence and humiliation — personal credit was not always warm. Albert Hirschman, *The Passions and the Interests* (1977, verified), records the 18th-century "doux commerce" thesis that markets soften manners and spread trust. Henrich et al., "In Search of Homo Economicus" (*AER* 2001, verified), found that market integration across 15 small-scale societies correlated with *more* fairness in experimental games.

**Reading.** For the author's point 8: personal credit was the market's origin (Muldrew, Graeber), but modern markets were deliberately rebuilt to need *less* belief between persons. The floor did not vanish; it moved from persons to systems. Whether that move depletes the author's "balances" or protects them from exploitation is exactly where Graeber/Muldrew and Fontaine/Hirschman disagree.

## B1. The eight points against published thought

The literature most strongly supports points 6 (drift through comfort) and 7 (gathering), and most strongly challenges points 2 (love as debt) and 3 (balances rather than hatred). Point 1 (origin) has the deepest philosophical support and the oldest counter-argument.

### Point 1 — Origin in one's own judgment

**Support.** Kant, "An Answer to the Question: What Is Enlightenment?" (1784, verified): immaturity is letting a book, a pastor or a doctor judge for you; *sapere aude*. Mill, *On Liberty* (1859, verified), ch. 3: one who lets the world choose his plan of life needs only the faculty of imitation. Benjamin's aura (1935–36, verified) locates value in origin. Charles Taylor, *The Ethics of Authenticity* (1991, verified), defends authenticity as a real moral ideal against its debased, self-indulgent forms. Hannah Arendt, *The Life of the Mind* (1978, verified), and *Eichmann in Jerusalem* (1963, verified), tie thoughtlessness — failure to think for oneself — to moral collapse.

**Complication.** Plato, *Phaedrus* (c. 370 BCE, verified), has Socrates argue that writing will weaken memory and give the appearance of wisdom without understanding — the same objection the author makes to AI, made against writing. Clark and Chalmers, "The Extended Mind" (*Analysis* 1998, verified), argue cognition legitimately extends into tools. MacIntyre, *After Virtue* (1981, verified), and Gadamer, *Truth and Method* (1960, verified), argue judgment always operates inside a tradition — nobody's reflection originates from nothing. Barthes, "The Death of the Author" (1967, verified), challenges origin as the source of meaning.

**Strongest objection.** Romance has always borrowed words. Rostand's *Cyrano de Bergerac* (1897, verified) is a love story built on ghostwritten letters; letter-writing manuals ("complete letter-writers") supplied love letters for centuries (probable as a general historical claim); greeting cards do so today. If borrowed words could carry romance when the sender *chose* and *sent* them, then origin may lie in the choosing and the risk of sending, not in composing. The author needs to say whether "the AI said…" fails because of where the words came from, or because nobody judged and stood behind them.

### Point 2 — Exchange as mutual bonds

**Support.** Marcel Mauss, *The Gift* (1925, verified): gifts create obligations to give, receive and reciprocate, and these obligations make society. Alvin Gouldner, "The Norm of Reciprocity" (*ASR* 1960, verified). Lewis Hyde, *The Gift* (1983, verified): gifts circulate and bind; commodities end relationships. Marshall Sahlins, *Stone Age Economics* (1972, verified): "generalised reciprocity" among intimates, where no one keeps accounts. Lien-sheng Yang, "The Concept of *Pao* as a Basis for Social Relations in China" (in Fairbank, ed., *Chinese Thought and Institutions*, 1957, probable). Ruth Benedict, *The Chrysanthemum and the Sword* (1946, verified), on Japanese *on* and *giri* as debts — though her wartime ethnography is widely criticised. Nietzsche, *On the Genealogy of Morality* (1887, verified), Essay II: guilt (*Schuld*) derives from debt (*Schulden*).

**Complication.** Graeber, *Debt* (2011, verified), argues that what sustains friendship is "baseline communism" — giving according to ability without accounts — and that once relations are framed as debts, they become payable, and paying a debt ends the relationship. He also notes debt is historically the language of domination. Derrida, *Given Time* (1991, verified): a true gift is impossible once it is recognised as creating a debt. Christian *agape* and Levinas's asymmetric responsibility (*Totality and Infinity*, 1961, verified) describe love that expects no return.

**Strongest objection.** Debt implies a creditor who can call it in, and a debtor who can be shamed; abusive relationships often run on manufactured indebtedness. A critic would say the author has described reciprocity but named it with the one financial term that implies power and default. The author may want Graeber's distinction: a bond that is *never meant to be settled* (Sahlins's generalised reciprocity) differs in kind from a debt that can be.

### Point 3 — Balances decreasing, not hatred increasing

**Support.** Putnam (2000, verified) and the social-capital literature (Coleman, "Social Capital in the Creation of Human Capital", *AJS* 1988, verified; Bourdieu, "The Forms of Capital", 1986, verified) treat trust and networks as stocks. US generalised trust has fallen on survey measures since the 1970s (General Social Survey; verified as a trend). Kierkegaard, *Two Ages / The Present Age* (1846, verified), describes a "levelling" age dominated by "the public" and chatter. Heidegger, *Being and Time* (1927, verified), describes *Gerede* (idle talk) — talk passed along without grasping its subject. Frankfurt, *On Bullshit* (2005, verified): speech indifferent to truth. Vosoughi, Roy and Aral, "The spread of true and false news online" (*Science* 2018, verified): false news spread farther and faster than true news on Twitter.

**Complication.** Hirschman, "Against Parsimony" (*Economics and Philosophy* 1984, probable on year), argues love and civic spirit are not like stocks: they grow with use and atrophy with disuse. That changes the model — balances fall from *non-use*, not from withdrawal. Affective polarisation research argues hatred *is* rising: Iyengar, Sood and Lelkes, "Affect, Not Ideology" (*POQ* 2012, verified); Boxell, Gentzkow and Shapiro, "Cross-Country Trends in Affective Polarization" (*Review of Economics and Statistics*, 2024, probable), find a strong rise in the US but not in most other countries. Robin Dunbar, *Grooming, Gossip and the Evolution of Language* (1996, verified), argues gossip — rumour — is how humans built bonds in the first place. Misinformation research (e.g., Altay, Berriche and Acerbi, "Misinformation on Misinformation", *Social Media + Society* 2023, probable) argues exposure to false news is small and concentrated.

**Strongest objection.** "Balances falling" and "hatred rising" may not be separable. If a balance can go negative, rising hatred *is* a falling balance. The author needs to say whether the balances have a floor at zero (indifference) or can go below it (enmity). Separately, Dunbar's point means rumour is not inherently corrosive: what may differ is whether gossip circulates among people who owe each other something.

### Point 4 — What is human: mistakes, inefficiency

**Support.** Arendt, *The Human Condition* (1958, verified): labour, work and action, with action — beginning something new among others — as distinctively human. Josef Pieper, *Leisure: The Basis of Culture* (1948, verified). Bertrand Russell, "In Praise of Idleness" (1932, verified). Frédéric Gros, *A Philosophy of Walking* (2009; English 2014, verified). Rebecca Solnit, *Wanderlust* (2000, verified). Hartmut Rosa, *Social Acceleration* (2005; English 2013, verified). Byung-Chul Han, *The Burnout Society* (2010; English 2015, verified). Max Weber's "iron cage" of instrumental rationality, *The Protestant Ethic* (1904–05, verified). George Ritzer, *The McDonaldization of Society* (1993, verified). Zhuangzi's useless tree, which survives because no carpenter wants it (Zhuangzi, ch. 1 and 4, verified). Richard Sennett, *The Craftsman* (2008, verified), on learning through error and resistance.

**Complication.** Keynes, "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren" (1930, verified), saw efficiency as the route to leisure. Judy Wajcman, *Pressed for Time* (2015, verified), shows the feeling of time scarcity is not simply caused by technology. The forty-minute walk is chosen; for many, inefficiency is imposed — no bus, long unpaid labour.

**Strongest objection.** Romanticising inefficiency is a privilege of those for whom efficiency already did its work. A critic will ask whether the forty-minute walk is romantic because it is slow, or because it is *chosen* and *spent on reflection*. If the latter, inefficiency is not the variable — freely given attention is.

### Point 5 — A test for AI: gathers or scatters, grows thought or replaces it

**Support.** Ivan Illich, *Tools for Conviviality* (1973, verified): tools are convivial if they enlarge people's autonomy and mutual relations, and manipulative beyond a threshold. Langdon Winner, "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" (*Daedalus* 1980, verified). Melvin Kranzberg, "Technology and History: 'Kranzberg's Laws'" (*Technology and Culture* 1986, verified): technology is neither good nor bad, nor is it neutral. Postman, *Technopoly* (1992, verified). Evidence on cognitive offloading: Risko and Gilbert, "Cognitive Offloading" (*Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 2016, verified); Sparrow, Liu and Wegner, "Google Effects on Memory" (*Science* 2011, verified, with later replication problems, probable). On generative AI: Lee et al., "The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking" (CHI 2025, probable) — self-reported reduced critical effort with high AI confidence; Bastani et al., "Generative AI Can Harm Learning" (2024 working paper; later published, probable) — unrestricted GPT-4 access improved practice performance but hurt later unaided exam scores; Kosmyna et al., "Your Brain on ChatGPT" (MIT Media Lab preprint, 2025, probable; small sample, not peer reviewed at release).

**Complication.** Clark and Chalmers (1998) again: offloading is how human cognition has always worked. Studies also find AI tutoring with guardrails improves learning (the same Bastani study's guarded condition; probable). The "time saved and spent with people" clause meets the rebound literature: Cowan (1983) and Wajcman (2015) show saved time is usually reabsorbed by rising standards or more work.

**Strongest objection.** The test is good, but almost no tool passes or fails it in itself; outcomes depend on institutions and use. The same model can scatter a family and gather a study group. A critic would ask the author to apply the test to *practices* (how AI is used, by whom, in what setting), not to AI as such.

### Point 6 — Drift through comfort

**Support.** Albert Borgmann, *Technology and the Character of Contemporary Life* (1984, verified): the "device paradigm" replaces engaging "focal things and practices" (the hearth, the family meal) with commodities that deliver the end without the effort, and we do not notice what goes. Marcuse (1964, verified): comfortable unfreedom. Scitovsky (1976, verified): comfort crowds out stimulation. Postman's foreword to *Amusing Ourselves to Death* (1985, verified): Huxley, not Orwell — we are undone by what we love. Huxley, *Brave New World* (1932, verified). Robert Nozick's experience machine, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974, verified). Daniel Pauly, "Anecdotes and the shifting baseline syndrome of fisheries" (*Trends in Ecology & Evolution* 1995, verified): each generation takes the degraded state it first saw as normal. Peter Kahn's "environmental generational amnesia" (*Technological Nature*, 2011, probable). Brickman and Campbell, "Hedonic relativism" (1971, verified): adaptation to comfort.

**On the myth itself.** In the *Odyssey*, Book 10, after Circe restores the men from swine they stay a full year feasting, and it is the companions who must remind Odysseus of home (verified as plot). The Lotus-Eaters of Book 9, who lose the wish to return, may fit "giving up even the sense of what we are losing" more exactly (verified as plot).

**Complication.** Critics of declinism (see Point 7) argue the sense of loss is itself generationally produced. Shifting baselines cut both ways: they explain lost awareness, but they also explain why every generation believes its youth was the last authentic era.

**Strongest objection.** If the drift erases the sense of loss, how does the author know what was lost? The argument must rest on evidence external to felt experience — time-use data, historical records — or it becomes unfalsifiable.

### Point 7 — Gathering made people human

**Support.** Arendt (1958, verified): plurality — that men, not Man, live on earth — is the condition of action and politics. Durkheim, *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912, verified): collective effervescence in gatherings creates the sacred and the social. Randall Collins, *Interaction Ritual Chains* (2004, verified). Victor Turner, *The Ritual Process* (1969, verified): communitas. Tocqueville, *Democracy in America* (1835/1840, verified): associations counter individualism. Habermas, *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere* (1962; English 1989, verified). Mercier and Sperber, *The Enigma of Reason* (2017, verified): reasoning evolved for argument among people and works best in group deliberation. Michael Tomasello, *A Natural History of Human Thinking* (2014, verified): shared intentionality as the root of human cognition. Aristotle, *Politics* I (verified): the human is a political animal; *Nicomachean Ethics* VIII–IX on friendship. Ubuntu ethics: Thaddeus Metz, "Toward an African Moral Theory" (*Journal of Political Philosophy* 2007, verified).

**Complication.** McPherson, Smith-Lovin and Brashears, "Social Isolation in America" (*ASR* 2006, verified), reported a sharp drop in confidants 1985–2004; Fischer (*ASR* 2009 comment, verified) and Paik and Sanchagrin (*ASR* 2013, probable) attribute much of it to survey artefacts. Claude Fischer, *Still Connected* (2011, verified): American personal ties were broadly stable 1970–2010. Wellman, "The Community Question" (*AJS* 1979, verified): community "liberated", not lost. danah boyd, *It's Complicated* (2014, verified): teens gather online because adults restricted their offline gathering. On the dark side of gathering: Le Bon, *The Crowd* (1895, verified); Cass Sunstein, "The Law of Group Polarization" (*Journal of Political Philosophy* 2002, verified); Kierkegaard's "the crowd is untruth" (verified as a theme).

**Strongest objection.** Raymond Williams, *The Country and the City* (1973, verified), traces an "escalator" of writers in every period mourning an organic community that was always one generation earlier. Svetlana Boym, *The Future of Nostalgia* (2001, verified), separates restorative nostalgia (rebuild the lost home) from reflective nostalgia (dwell on longing itself). A critic would say the gathering narrative is the oldest declinism in sociology (Tönnies 1887 onward) and must show its decline with data, not with feeling.

### Point 8 — The market's floor is mutual belief

**Support.** "Credit" from Latin *credere*, to believe (verified). Muldrew (1998, verified); Graeber (2011, verified). Geoffrey Ingham, *The Nature of Money* (2004, verified): money as a social relation of credit and debt. Simmel (1900, verified): money rests on trust in the community. Keynes's "animal spirits" (*General Theory*, 1936, verified); Akerlof and Shiller, *Animal Spirits* (2009, verified). Kenneth Arrow, "Gifts and Exchanges" (*Philosophy & Public Affairs* 1972, verified): virtually every commercial transaction has an element of trust. Fukuyama, *Trust* (1995, verified).

**Complication.** Giddens (1990), Luhmann (1979) and Zucker (1986) describe modern markets as resting on **system trust**, deliberately designed to not need belief between persons. Donald MacKenzie, *An Engine, Not a Camera* (2006, verified), shows financial models actively shape the markets they describe (performativity), so the "complex mathematical structure" is not only a superstructure. Nakamoto's Bitcoin paper (2008, verified) is an explicit attempt to build money that requires no trust in a counterparty.

**Strongest objection.** The market's floor may now be belief in *institutions and code*, not belief between persons. That makes point 8 a historical claim (true of the origin) rather than a structural one (true now). The author needs to say whether system trust is a thinner form of the same balance, or a different asset.

## B2. What the literature has that the eight points lack

The biggest gap is historical: much of the literature holds that **romantic love is itself a product of modernization**, which the eight points treat only as its enemy. The other gaps are concepts the eight points come close to but do not name.

1. **Romance as a modern invention.** Lawrence Stone, *The Family, Sex and Marriage in England 1500–1800* (1977, verified, contested on details); Niklas Luhmann, *Love as Passion* (1982; English 1986, verified); Stephanie Coontz, *Marriage, a History* (2005, verified); Denis de Rougemont, *Love in the Western World* (1939, verified); Giddens, *The Transformation of Intimacy* (1992, verified), on the "pure relationship"; Habermas (1962) on letters and the novel creating the intimate private sphere. Reading: postal systems, privacy, the novel and individual choice of partner *produced* the romance the work mourns. The ontology may need to separate what modernization gave romance (1750–1950) from what later waves took.
2. **Forgiveness and write-off.** Arendt (1958, verified) names forgiving and promising as the two human faculties that make action bearable: forgiveness releases from the past, promise binds the future. Graeber (2011) on debt jubilees. Reading: a bond model without forgiveness has no write-off, restructuring or amnesty — the instruments real credit systems need to avoid collapse.
3. **Promise.** Arendt (1958); Nietzsche, *Genealogy* II, on man as the animal entitled to make promises (verified). The mobile-phone literature shows planning replaced by micro-coordination. A promise is the purest person-originated bond, and the eight points do not name it.
4. **Attention as gift.** Simone Weil, letter to Joë Bousquet (1942, probable on date), calls attention the rarest and purest form of generosity; Iris Murdoch, *The Sovereignty of Good* (1970, verified), on attention as moral vision. Jenny Odell, *How to Do Nothing* (2019, verified). Reading: the forty-minute walk and the hand-written letter may share a common term — undivided attention — rather than inefficiency.
5. **Uncontrollability and risk.** Rosa, *The Uncontrollability of the World* (2018; English 2020, verified) and *Resonance* (2016; English 2019, verified): resonance needs a world that answers back and cannot be ordered. Badiou (2009): love as a risk of encountering difference. Dreyfus (2001): commitment requires risk. Reading: an AI or feed that always answers, always pleases, removes the risk of rejection — and with it, the stakes that make romance.
6. **Otherness.** Byung-Chul Han, *The Agony of Eros* (2012; English 2017, verified): a culture of sameness and self-optimisation makes the experience of the Other, and so eros, vanish. Levinas (1961). Reading: personalisation is the technical form of Han's "hell of the same".
7. **Embodiment and place.** Merleau-Ponty, *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945, verified); Edward Relph, *Place and Placelessness* (1976, verified); Marc Augé, *Non-Places* (1992, verified). The eight points say "medium does not matter"; much of the literature says the body and the place matter a great deal.
8. **Recognition.** Hegel; Axel Honneth, *The Struggle for Recognition* (1992; English 1995, verified): love, rights and esteem as three forms of recognition. This could supply the "what is owed" in the author's bonds.
9. **Care.** Joan Tronto, *Moral Boundaries* (1993, verified); Nel Noddings, *Caring* (1984, verified). Care is asymmetric — a parent, a nurse — and does not fit an exchange-of-bonds model.
10. **Solitude vs loneliness.** Arendt, *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951, verified), separates solitude (being with oneself) from loneliness (being deserted by all, including oneself). Turkle (2015) argues phones erode solitude. The author's "solitary reflection is romantic" needs this distinction.
11. **Ritual.** Han, *The Disappearance of Rituals* (2019; English 2020, verified); Collins (2004). Ritual is gathering with form, repeated; the eight points have gathering but not form.
12. **Forgetting.** Viktor Mayer-Schönberger, *Delete* (2009, verified): digital memory ends social forgetting. A permanent record changes what a bond, a mistake or a letter means.
13. **Waiting and anticipation.** The letter's delay, the railway's slowness, the planned meeting (Schivelbusch; Han, *The Scent of Time*, 2009; English 2017, verified). Desire lives in the interval; instant delivery removes it.
14. **Who lost what.** Feminist and labour histories show losses and gains fell unequally: Cowan (1983) on women's work; Coontz (2005) on marriage's escape from economic compulsion; Fontaine (2014) on personal credit as domination. Not everyone had the romance being lost.
15. **What fills the space.** Wellman's networked individualism, boyd's online gathering, Fischer's stable ties. The literature often finds **substitution** rather than pure loss; the author's model may need a term for what replaced each lost thing, and whether the replacement is thinner.

## C. Recurring concepts (my proposals)

Eighteen concepts recur across the sources; all are my proposals for an ontology, not findings. They cluster into four groups: **origination**, **bond**, **occasion** and **erosion**.
