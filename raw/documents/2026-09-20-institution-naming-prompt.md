# LONGING — Institution and Index Naming, with Collision Check

Purpose: generate candidate names for two fictional institutions and four indices, and report the real-world collision risk of each.

---

# 1. What this round is for

A fictional research institution covers a market in human practices — handwritten letters, unplanned calls, waiting without contact, solitude, and similar — across three national markets: Japan, Korea and the United States.

Real flagship indices are named after four different kinds of authority, and that is the design principle here: **the name records who held the right to measure.**

- KOSPI — a country. The state counted it.
- Nikkei — a newspaper. *Nihon Keizai Shimbun* computes and publishes it.
- S&P — a ratings agency. A private firm's name became the market's yardstick.
- NASDAQ — the quotation system itself. A machine's name became a market's.

Each market's index in this work is therefore published by a different fictional institution, so that the naming structure carries the same information the real one does. Late in the work's history the three are absorbed into one international composite published by the covering institution — which is the convergence of these markets told through their names.

Six naming objects are needed:

1. **A Japanese evening or economic newspaper** that computes and publishes the Japanese index.
2. **A United States ratings house**, in the two-surname form that `Standard & Poor's` and `Dow Jones` established.
3. **The Korean index**, in the state-issued acronym form of KOSPI.
4. **The Japanese index**, in the `<publisher> Average` form.
5. **The United States index**, in the `<ratings house> <number>` form.
6. **Tickers** for all three plus the global composite.

---

# 2. Constraints

**Register.** Dry, institutional, contract-term English. The work's existing vocabulary is of this kind: `BEARER BOND`, `BLIND TRUST`, `STANDARD RETURN`, `Outstanding Coverage Value`, `Coverage Discontinued`. Nothing may wink at the audience. A name that is clever about what the work is *about* destroys the premise, because the institution does not know it is in an artwork.

**One forbidden word.** The word `romance`, and direct synonyms for it, must not appear in any name. The work's subject is stated as the conditions of human experience that modernization is removing, and it deliberately refuses romance as a category label.

**The global composite is already named** `LONGING COMPOSITE` and is not open for proposal. It is given so you can pitch the other names against it.

**Plausibility over invention.** These should read as institutions that could have existed in 1996. A Japanese paper name should be formed the way Japanese paper names are formed; a ratings house should be two plausible surnames of the era.

---

# 3. What this round is NOT for

Do not propose names for the work itself, the covering institution, the securities, or the unit. All are settled.

Do not explain the artistic merit of your proposals at length. One line each.

Do not present a name as clear of collisions unless you have grounds. See Section 4.

---

# 4. Rules of engagement — the collision check

This is the part that matters more than the names.

For every candidate you propose, state:

- whether an entity of that name **exists or has existed** — company, publication, index, fund, product, or well-known fictional entity
- if so, what and where, and how prominent
- whether the name is likely to be a **registered trademark** in Japan, Korea, the United States, or the EU, and in which classes it would matter
- whether a **domain** in the obvious form is likely to be taken
- your **confidence**, and the basis for it

**State your limits honestly.** You are not a trademark search. You cannot see a live register. Say so once, and then mark every collision judgement with the basis you actually used — recall of known entities, naming-convention inference, or explicit uncertainty. A candidate marked *unknown, would need a register search* is a usable answer. A candidate asserted clear and later found to be a real newspaper is not.

**Prefer to reject.** If a name is even plausibly occupied, say so and keep it out of the recommended tier. Generating twenty candidates of which four are clean is a better outcome than twelve of which several are quietly taken.

**Japanese and Korean names.** Give them in the original script, in romanisation, and with a literal gloss, so that a non-speaker can see what the name says.

---

# 5. Required output

## Section 1 — The Japanese publisher

Six to ten candidates. For each: the name in script, romanisation, literal gloss, one line on why the form is plausible for a paper of that kind, and the full collision report from Section 4.

## Section 2 — The United States ratings house

Six to ten candidates in the two-surname form, with the same collision report. Note any candidate whose surnames evoke a real firm even if the pair is not itself taken.

## Section 3 — The three index names

For each market, three to five candidates in the structure named in Section 1 of this prompt. Include the constituent-count convention where the form requires one, and say what the number should be a function of.

## Section 4 — Tickers

Candidates for all four series, including the global composite. Check each against real ticker symbols and index codes on the relevant exchanges, and say which are occupied.

## Section 5 — Names you rejected

List candidates you generated and discarded, with the reason. Collisions belong here, and so do names that were clever at the work's expense.

## Section 6 — Refusals and residual risk

State what you could not check. Then state which candidates you believe would require a professional clearance search before use, and which are low enough risk to proceed on.

---

A name that turns out to be a real company is worse than no name. Where you are unsure, say unsure.
